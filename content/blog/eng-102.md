In copper wires, the difference between **signal** and **power** is not the wave itself, but **its purpose, current, and impedance.**

Both power and electrical signals travel along wires as electromagnetic waves through the copper's electrical field, but they are handled completely differently:

**Power vs. Signal on a Wire**

* **Power (Energy Delivery):**
  * **Goal:** Maximize energy transfer to do mechanical or thermal work (e.g., spinning a pump motor).
  * **Current:** High (amperes).
  * **Impedance:** Very low source and line resistance to avoid burning voltage off as heat ($P = I^2R$).
  * **Waveform:** Usually steady DC (0 Hz) or a low-frequency power sine wave (50/60 Hz).

* **Signal (Information Delivery):**
  * **Goal:** Convey a state, command, or data stream ($0$ or $1$) to an input that reads it.
  * **Current:** Negligible (microamps to a few milliamps). An ESP32 input pin has extremely high internal resistance (megaohms), meaning it drinks virtually zero current—it simply senses whether the electric potential is at 0V or 3.3V.
  * **Amplitude:** Fixed logic levels (e.g., standard 3.3V or 5V pulses). It isn't necessarily smaller than battery voltage; rather, it carries almost **no physical energy**.


**What About Over the Air (RF)?**

When an ESP32 broadcasts Wi-Fi or Bluetooth, the signal is converted by the antenna into a true free-space **electromagnetic wave** (radio frequency at 2.4 GHz). In that context:

* It is identical in nature to light, X-rays, or microwaves—just at a specific frequency.
* The amplitude (field strength) is indeed tiny: the ESP32 transmits around 20 dBm (~100 milliwatts), and by the time that wave reaches your router, it has attenuated down to fractions of a microwatt.

You are thinking of **characteristic impedance** ($Z_0$), which is indeed an inherent geometric property of the medium (like a 50 $\Omega$ coaxial cable or a 100 $\Omega$ twisted pair).

When engineers talk about the impedance difference between power and signal circuits, they are referring to **source impedance and load impedance**—how the circuit is designed at its ends to manage energy versus information.

---

1. Power Delivery: Minimum Impedance Everywhere

In a power system (like your 3S battery driving the 20W pump), the goal is **pure energy transfer with zero waste**.

* **Source Impedance (Battery):** Must be as close to **$0\ \Omega$** as possible. A healthy 18650 cell has an internal resistance of only around $0.02\ \Omega$ ($20\ \text{m}\Omega$).
* **Load Impedance (Pump Motor):** Low (around $6\ \Omega$ to pull $2\text{A}$ at $12\text{V}$).
* **Why:** If the source or trace impedance were high, current flowing through it would drop voltage and turn your battery power into unwanted heat ($P = I^2R$) before it ever reached the pump.

---

2. Logic Signals: Mismatched, High-Impedance Receivers

In digital logic (like the ESP32 pin reading a button or driving a MOSFET gate), the goal is **sensing a voltage level, not moving power**.

* **Load Impedance (ESP32 GPIO Input / MOSFET Gate):** Designed to be **enormous**—often **$10\ \text{M}\Omega$ to $100\ \text{M}\Omega$** or more.
* **Why:** Because the input impedance is so high, virtually **zero current flows** ($I = V / R$).
* You can sense that the line is at "3.3V" without loading down or draining the circuit that created the voltage.
* The tiny tactile buttons in your kit don't melt because they only switch a few microamps into that high-impedance input.

3. High-Frequency Signals: Characteristic Impedance ($Z_0$)

This is where the property of the medium *does* come in.

When a signal changes so fast that the wire acts like a transmission line (such as the 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi trace on the ESP32, or high-speed USB data lines):

* The geometry of the trace, its width, and the fiberglass PCB material define a fixed **characteristic impedance** (typically $50\ \Omega$).
* Here, both the transmitter and receiver are intentionally matched to that exact $50\ \Omega$ value. If they don't match, the electromagnetic wave reflects backward off the end of the wire, creating echoes that corrupt the data.

> Ignore [this](https://github.com/JAlcocerT/poc/tree/main/physics-stationary-wave) and fry your FPV VTX like i did

**In summary:** While the wire itself has a characteristic wave impedance, **power circuits prioritize near-zero impedance** to push current without heat, whereas **logic signal inputs use massive impedance** so they can read a voltage without drawing any current at all.

Yes, they are fundamentally different in how they travel, how they represent data, and how you must design the circuit for them.

| Characteristic | Logic Signals (GPIO, I2C, UART, SPI) | High-Frequency RF Signals (Wi-Fi, Zigbee, ELRS) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Physical Medium** | Confined entirely inside a copper wire or PCB trace. | Radiated out through an antenna into open space as true electromagnetic radiation (radio waves). |
| **Frequencies** | DC to tens of megahertz (0 Hz to ~40 MHz). | Gigahertz range: 2.4 GHz (Wi-Fi, BLE, Zigbee, ELRS) or 868/915 MHz (Sub-GHz ELRS). |
| **Waveform** | Square waves: sharp transitions between discrete DC voltage levels (0V and 3.3V). | High-frequency continuous sinusoidal carrier waves modulated with data. |
| **How Data is Encoded** | **Direct levels:** 0V = `0`, 3.3V = `1`. | **Modulation:** Small shifts in the carrier wave's phase, frequency (e.g., LoRa chirp in ELRS), or amplitude. |
| **How the Circuit Sees It** | A simple DC voltage potential. Traces act like plain conductive lines; wire length rarely matters at low speeds. | A **transmission line** and **wave phenomenon**. Trace geometry, ground planes, and wave reflections dominate circuit performance. |

---

Why the Difference Matters on a Board

**1. The Wave vs. Wire Threshold**

* For a **logic signal** running at modest speeds, the electrical pulse travels across the board far faster than the signal changes state. The entire wire reaches 3.3V practically instantaneously. You do not need to worry about wave reflection; you only care that the wire makes contact.
* For **RF signals** (like 2.4 GHz Wi-Fi), the wavelength is only about **12.5 cm in air** (and around **6 cm inside PCB fiberglass**). If your trace is even 1 or 2 cm long, different points along that trace are at completely different phases of the wave simultaneously.

**2. Antenna & Impedance Constraints**

* If you hook a stray wire or a loose breadboard jumper to a logic GPIO pin, the ESP32 still reads the 3.3V level fine.
* If you hook a loose breadboard jumper to an RF output (like the antenna pin on an ESP32 or an ELRS transmitter), the wave will reflect back off the wire, fail to radiate, and can easily overheat or blow the radio frequency power amplifier (PA). RF paths require an exact **50 $\Omega$ controlled-impedance trace** directly into a tuned antenna.

In short: **logic signals** are basic DC voltages switched on and off through wires to talk across a board, while **Wi-Fi, ELRS, and Zigbee** are high-frequency radio waves engineered to detach from conductors and fly through the air.

The **XL4015** is a higher-power step-down (buck) converter.

While the LM2596 and MP1584EN max out around 2A to 3A, the XL4015 is built for **up to 5A continuous output** (with appropriate heatsinking) and frequently comes in variants with **adjustable current limiting** (constant current / constant voltage).

Here is how the XL4015 slots directly into your comparison:

| Feature | LM2596 (Blue Module) | MP1584EN (Green Module) | XL4015 (Common Red/Blue Module) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Size** | Bulky (~43 × 21 mm) | Ultra-compact (~22 × 17 mm) | Largest footprint (~54 × 24 mm to 65 × 36 mm) |
| **Max Practical Current** | ~1.5A continuous (3A peak with heatsink) | ~1.5A continuous (3A short burst) | **4A continuous, 5A max** (with heatsink) |
| **Efficiency & Tech** | Older bipolar tech, 150 kHz | Modern CMOS, ~1.5 MHz | Modern MOSFET-based, 180 kHz, ~96% peak efficiency |
| **Adjustment Dial** | Multi-turn pot (CV only) | Single-turn pot (CV only, very twitchy) | **Multi-turn pot** (often **dual pots** for both CV and CC) |
| **Max Input** | ~40V | ~28V | **~36V to 38V** (dropout ~1.5V) |
| **Current Limiting (CC)** | No (voltage regulation only) | No (voltage regulation only) | **Yes, on dual-pot models** (can charge batteries or drive LEDs) |


* **Real 4A–5A Muscle:** The LM2596 and MP1584EN will thermal-throttle or fail if pushed past 2A for prolonged periods. The XL4015 uses an internal low-$R_{DS(on)}$ power MOSFET instead of an old bipolar transistor, meaning significantly lower heat dissipation at 3A–4A.

* **True Constant Current (CC / CV):** Most common XL4015 boards feature two multi-turn trimmers (and often three onboard LEDs: Constant Current, Constant Voltage, and Fully Charged). This makes the XL4015 a functional, benchtop-style CC/CV charger for lithium packs, lead-acid batteries, or high-power LED strings, whereas the LM2596 and MP1584EN simply shut down or brown out under overcurrent.

* **Thermal Overhead:** Because it is designed to push up to 75W, almost all XL4015 boards ship with or require an aluminum stick-on heatsink over the IC.

| **Hover Thermal Loss ($I^2R$)** | Efficiency | Battery heating loss during static hover | **~1.5 W** (~1.1% of hover) | < 2% (negligible) |
| **Ideal Induced Hover Power ($P_i$)** | Aerodynamics | Theoretical minimum power required to hover under Momentum Theory | **35.6 W** | — |
| **Hover Figure of Merit ($FoM$)** | Aerodynamics | Aerodynamic rotor efficiency relative to theoretical limit ($P_i / P_{\text{hover}}$) | **27.4%** | 25% – 35% (mini-rotor systems) |
| **Hover Motor RPM** | Propulsion | Rotor speed needed to generate 550 g of static thrust | *Missing* (need Bidirectional DShot log) | **11,000 – 12,200 RPM** |
| **Hover Throttle %** | Control | Throttle stick position required to maintain steady altitude | *Missing* (read in Blackbox log) | **28% – 34%** |
